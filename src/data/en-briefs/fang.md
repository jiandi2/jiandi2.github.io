Hanming Fang is the Joseph M. Cohen Term Professor of Economics at the University of Pennsylvania, a former chair of Penn’s department, and a Fellow of the Econometric Society. He received the Arrow Award in health economics. This conversation does not begin with those titles. It begins with a student who almost became a physicist.

Fang entered Fudan through a national physics olympiad. In the late 1980s, with scientific exchanges constrained, he chose a field that still felt open to China’s reform: world economy, a new department built to teach Western economics rather than recycle the old curriculum. The accident of that choice, he argues, was also an accident of an era. The interview then turns to the American job market — what has changed, and what has not — and to the quieter problem of how a young economist learns to last.

Two judgments stay with the reader. First, research taste is formed early, often before one has language for it; the first paper is less a product than a self-portrait. Second, the market’s surface rituals shift — the slides, the signals, the etiquette of a flyout — but the underlying test does not: can you make other people care about a question you have made your own? Fang is unusually concrete here. He talks as a former department chair, not as a mascot of success.

That is why the conversation is not only a China story. It is about professional formation in a discipline that pretends to be global while remaining stubbornly local in its hiring, its journals, and its luck. A scholar who has never written on China will still recognize the problem: how to last, once the first placement is over.
