Zheng Song is Wei Lun Professor of Economics and department chair at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, a Fellow of the Econometric Society, and a former faculty member at Chicago Booth. In the international literature on the Chinese economy, one paper is almost unavoidable: “Growing Like China,” published in the *American Economic Review*. This conversation is about how that kind of paper becomes possible.

Song was among the first Chinese students at Stockholm’s Institute for International Economic Studies — a small, almost monastic place where people worried about institutions and political economy without much career anxiety. That culture, he says, licensed a kind of purity: follow the question, not the market. The method that followed looks like detective work. Local-government balance sheets, archival numbers, institutional details that a representative-agent model would have no room for — these are not ornaments. They are the model.

The claim is sharper than “China is special.” If you take institutions seriously enough to measure them, China stops being a case and starts being evidence. The facts of Chinese growth can discipline general theory, not merely illustrate it. Song’s reputation as a data detective — local-government balance sheets, numbers recovered from archives — is in this sense a theoretical method. The fact has to be found before it can embarrass a model.

That is the invitation this conversation extends to scholars who do not work on China. The question is not whether to study a particular country. It is whether your theory can survive contact with a large economy whose institutions you have not already assumed away. A pure academic culture, he suggests, is what makes that kind of stubbornness possible.
