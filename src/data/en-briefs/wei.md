Shang-Jin Wei is N.T. Wang Professor at Columbia Business School and the School of International and Public Affairs. He has been chief economist of the Asian Development Bank, and before that served at the IMF, including as a deputy director in the Research Department. The interview moves between two rooms that economists usually keep apart: the study, where a question has to be true, and the decision room, where a question has to be usable.

Wei belongs to an early cohort that left China through the Chow test — he likes to say he physically passed it. The intellectual signature is the opposite of a grand theory. A good question, he argues, often grows out of life: the sex ratio and the saving puzzle, which became the competitive-saving motive; the “missing imports”; officials’ housing. Measurement is a form of argument. So is general equilibrium, which he treats not as a technical taste but as a public responsibility: partial truths can mislead a ministry as easily as a seminar.

The career is not offered as a model to copy. Few academics will become chief economists of a multilateral bank. The transferable claim is smaller and harder. If you cannot explain how your mechanism would change a decision, you may not yet understand the mechanism. Wei is equally impatient with the opposite error: taking a partial-equilibrium cleverness into a room that has to live in general equilibrium.

That is why English-speaking scholars, including those who have never written on China, belong in this series. The tension between research and public reason is not a Chinese predicament. It is the predicament of the discipline whenever it is taken seriously. The invitation is to sit for the same kind of conversation — not to comment on China from a distance.
