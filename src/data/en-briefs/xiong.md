Wei Xiong is the John H. Scully ’66 Professor of Finance and Professor of Economics at Princeton, and academic dean of the School of Management and Economics at CUHK-Shenzhen. With José Scheinkman he wrote “Overconfidence and Speculative Bubbles,” a paper the field has not been able to put down. He spent six years as a co-editor of the *Journal of Finance*. He has also spent a career trying to keep a clear head about a subject — bubbles — that is almost designed to confuse.

The origin story is physical in the literal sense. The University of Science and Technology of China, a Columbia physics master’s, a glimpse of big science at CERN, then a turn to finance at Duke. Literature, he says, became a mirror: the same human materials that show up in speculative episodes. The China work is not an application of that insight but a test of it. The warrant bubble; the financial system; the claim he presses in conversation: the useful frame is not “more government or less government,” but market-in-state.

Together with Hanming Fang and others he helped found VoxChina, on the premise that the international profession still does not know how to read the Chinese economy. The interview treats that ignorance as a scientific problem, not a public-relations one. His *Handbook of China’s Financial System* is offered in the same spirit: a map, not a brochure.

A scholar who works on asset prices, behavioral finance, or political economy will hear a familiar question in an unfamiliar register. How do you study a phenomenon that feeds on belief, including the belief of the researcher? Clarity, here, is not a personality trait. It is a method — and, in Xiong’s telling, the only way to spend a lifetime on bubbles without being captured by them.
